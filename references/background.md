# Background: why ambition is hard

From the essay's closing section. This is context for the techniques, not a procedure.

Humans have always been held back by what they believe is possible, and usually trust themselves only in very narrow disciplines. Michelangelo famously did not want to paint the Sistine Chapel, because he was a sculptor, not a painter.

Psychology calls this **self-efficacy**: people with high self-efficacy believe more in their own abilities, and in turn accomplish more. Research suggests it can be influenced. Locke and Latham reviewed 35 years of goal-setting research and concluded that people given specific, hard goals perform better than those told simply to do their best. Patrick Collison's "Fast" list records what people have accomplished when given audacious goals and tight deadlines.

Documented ways to accomplish hard things, as the essay describes them:

- **Believing it is possible.** In 1939, George Dantzig arrived late to a statistics class at Berkeley, copied two problems from the blackboard, and handed in solutions, thinking they were homework. They were two unsolved problems in statistics.
- **Watching someone else do it.** Roger Bannister broke the four-minute mile in May 1954 and was the only person to have done so for 46 days. The essay notes that the two-hour marathon has followed a similar pattern.
- **Permission to fail.** *The Inner Game of Tennis* argues that you do not play your best when you try very, very hard. Linux was announced as "just a hobby", not going to be big and professional like GNU.

The essay's conclusion: self-efficacy is not a new problem, but it matters more now, because capabilities are advancing every few weeks. The author asks readers to share further techniques for being more ambitious.
